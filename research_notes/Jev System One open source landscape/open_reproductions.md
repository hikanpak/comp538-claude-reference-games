# Open reproductions and open alternatives to TypeSafe Jev (state of 1 November 2026)

Method note. Every repository statistic below (stars, forks, creation date, last push, licence field) was read from the GitHub REST API on 1 November 2026 through an authenticated `gh api repos/<owner>/<repo>` call, and every Hugging Face download and like count from `https://huggingface.co/api/models/<id>` on the same day. Hugging Face "downloads" is the trailing-30-day count. READMEs were read in full from the repositories themselves. A claim marked "search summary" was seen only in a web-search result and not on the project's own page. Numbers marked "self-reported" come from the project's own README or model card and were not reproduced.

## Which projects exist? Verification of the reported names and further projects

### Takeaway
All eight reported names exist, but three are not what the brief implies: SiliconLabAI/OpenJev is a Node playground with no model of its own (it wraps Mapika/decider), LitJev is a training-free inference wrapper, and OpenSysOne is a 16.5M-parameter adapter whose reproduction guide covers only inference. The ecosystem is far larger than the list in the brief: the awesome-decision-models list alone has about 50 open models and 10 training-free techniques, and a GitHub search adds at least CLM-8B, AgentJev-0.6B, opendecider, LLM2Jev, rlcd-lite and mini-jev, which the list does not carry.

### Inferences
- "Reproduction" is used loosely in this ecosystem. No project reproduces Jev's training method, because TypeSafe has not published it. Projects reproduce the interface (state plus Noul, Choice and Score questions, `POST /v1/systemone`), and a minority also copy Jev's outputs by distillation (JEV-27B, mini-jev).
- The brief's list is weighted toward low-visibility projects (OpenSysOne with 1 like, opendecider with 37 stars, LitJev with 47 stars). The projects with large communities and full training code are Kev, Laya, Strands Decider, decider and NanoJev.

### Gaps
- PyPI pages for `decider-ai`, `laya`, `opendecider` and `contrastive-lm` were not opened, so release histories and download counts from PyPI are missing.
- jiwo, foreman, open-alternative-jev, OpenJev-8B and jev-agent-risk-gate were not read beyond search listings.
- The Chinese README of the awesome list and the companion awesome-jev lists (yibie/awesome-jev with 2,521 stars, kydlikebtc/awesome-jev claiming 1,207 resources) were not cross-checked for further models.

## Per-project technical detail: base, licence, release, recipe, readout, calibration and accuracy

### Takeaway
The projects split into three groups. Trained one-pass models with a pointer or slot head and released training code (Kev, Strands Decider, decider, Jeeves, NanoJev, AgentJev, Laya), trained models with no training code (JEV-27B, OpenThai-SystemOne, opendecider, OpenSysOne, openjev/openjev), and training-free readouts over a frozen LLM (LitJev, LLM2Jev, AnyJev, SemIf). Only decider documents an RL stage whose reward comes from environment outcomes and whose loss acts on the served one-pass distribution, and only Jeeves, Laya, rlcd-lite and mini-jev ship RL training code.

### Cited Findings

Mapika/decider (the closest match to the course project):

- Models: decider-2b v12 (Qwen3.5-2B-Base, 1.9B, 32k context), decider-4b v3 (Qwen3.5-4B-Base, 4.2B), decider-0.8b, decider-35b-a3b (Qwen3.5-35B-A3B-Base), plus Gemma-4 variants (decider-12b, decider-12b-vision, decider-31b) and two `decider-chat` configs that are stock checkpoints with a fitted temperature. All Apache-2.0. — [decider README](https://github.com/Mapika/decider)
- Readout: one prompt with one answer slot per question, one forward pass, hidden state at each slot projected onto one label token per option (A–J, then K–Z and two-letter tokens up to 255 options), softmax over the valid options at a fitted temperature. Choice takes 2 to 255 options, Score 2 to 10 levels, Noul is P(yes). — [decider README](https://github.com/Mapika/decider)
- SFT recipe and data are released: `decider/data/` converts about 95 public datasets plus data labelled by a local Qwen3.5-27B teacher, `decider/train.py` is a cross-entropy fine-tune, and "Nothing was distilled from Jev". One epoch is 1.47M examples and 455M tokens, 5.3 h on one GH200, and reproduces the released supervised weights within noise. — [decider README](https://github.com/Mapika/decider)
- Calibration: `python -m decider.calibrate` fits per-type temperatures by NLL; `evaluate.py` reports accuracy, NLL, Brier, ECE and AURC per task. — [decider README](https://github.com/Mapika/decider)
- RL stage (v8 to v10), from docs/RL.md: 384 optimizer steps with rewards "only from outcomes", trained on "the served distribution itself" (letter logits at the slot, softmax at the stored temperature 1.30), with no separate policy head. Environments: 16 live MiniWoB++ click tasks (+1 if the task's checker reports success, −1 otherwise), exact 4x4 minesweeper, a slippery 5x5 grid, and draws from bags of known composition. — [decider docs/RL.md](https://github.com/Mapika/decider/blob/main/docs/RL.md)
- RL loss terms: (1) actor, coefficient 0.1, PPO clipped surrogate (clip 0.2) with terminal return and a leave-one-replicate-out baseline over 4 repeats; (2) belief, coefficient 0.2, a log score of the model's one-pass prediction of what happens next against the exact law, read in two option orders and averaged ("a proper scoring rule"); (3) rendering consistency, coefficient 1.0, KL across reversed order and schema-first layouts; (4) retention, coefficient 0.7, KL(v8 ‖ student) on 8 replayed supervised rows, with a fallback step when the mean exceeds 0.01 nats or a row exceeds 0.05 nats (94 of 576 steps). AdamW, peak learning rate 1e-6, cosine over 576 steps, 96 transitions per iteration. — [decider docs/RL.md](https://github.com/Mapika/decider/blob/main/docs/RL.md)
- RL selection used six gates against v8 (sampled win +0.02, greedy win not lower, belief excess −0.02 nats, general NLL at most +0.005, general accuracy at most −0.002, rendering unanimity at most −0.01). Four arms ran in parallel on four GPUs. A first attempt at learning rate 2e-6 drifted (KL up to 5.8 nats on single rows) and failed the gates. — [decider docs/RL.md](https://github.com/Mapika/decider/blob/main/docs/RL.md)
- RL results (self-reported, 95% intervals): live browser tasks sampled play 83.0% to 93.2%, the six never-rewarded tasks 72.9% to 91.7%, belief excess 0.473 to 0.219 nats, click-outcome log score −0.349 to −0.034, and the 5,252-row OpenJev fixture 64.1% to 63.3% (−0.8). — [decider docs/RL.md](https://github.com/Mapika/decider/blob/main/docs/RL.md)
- The RL training code "lives in a separate research repository" and "is not in this package yet", because it needs a live Chrome with MiniWoB++, the exact game environments and four GPUs. The package does contain `decider/games/` ("ten text games + Super Mario Bros behind the same interface, imitation and PPO"). — [decider README](https://github.com/Mapika/decider); [decider docs/RL.md](https://github.com/Mapika/decider/blob/main/docs/RL.md)
- Third-party standings quoted by decider (read 2026-09-29): Decision Index v0.2.1, chance-corrected, decider-2b 28.97 (rank 37, ECE 0.077), decider-4b 40.70 (rank 19, ECE 0.084), Jev 57.91. JevBench v1.5.2, decider-4b v2 rank 7 with score 71.3 and calibration axis 85.6, decider-2b rank 28 with 45.1. — [decider README](https://github.com/Mapika/decider)
- Stated limits: decider-2b v10's top-label ECE on JevBench's public hard items is 0.31 (v11 0.18), "One pass cannot do multi-step arithmetic", "Rules written into the question are not followed at this size", English only. — [decider README](https://github.com/Mapika/decider)

Kev (jaredpalmer/kev):

- Each checkpoint is a rank-16 LoRA adapter and a small pointer head on a Qwen3.5 Base model (Kev-0.8B, 4B, 9B). The pointer head scores each option's `</opt>` hidden state against the question's `<decide>` hidden state, then softmax. Kev-27B fine-tunes every weight of Qwen3.8-27B (51 GB of bf16 weights). — [Kev README](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)
- Training uses cross-entropy on the correct answer. The base set `decision-v7` has 10,000 examples from ten public datasets, 896 generated policy examples and 1,680 examples from 60 rule structures, two epochs at LoRA rank 16, learning rate 1e-4 (0.8B) or 5e-5 (4B, 9B). Optional `--perm_kl` and `--ord_w` losses exist but are unused in the releases. "No Jev outputs were used for training." — [Kev README](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)
- Calibration is a stored temperature per checkpoint (Kev-4B 2.41, Kev-0.8B 2.35, Kev-9B 2.19, Kev-27B 1.32). — [Kev README](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)
- Results (self-reported, development / test): on "new sources" Kev-4B accuracy 0.817 / 0.838 and Brier 0.269 / 0.242, Kev-27B 0.851 / 0.889 and Brier 0.225 / 0.156, Jev 0.857 and Brier 0.211 on development only. The benchmark reports accuracy, Brier, calibration error, automatable share at a 5% error budget, option-order changes and question isolation. — [Kev README](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)
- Fine-tuning path: `kev.train --init_from jaredpalmer/kev-4b` continues the released adapter and head. On an example support workload (1,050 generated records, 15 minutes on an H100) Kev-4B went from 67.7% to 73.6% accuracy (self-reported). — [Kev README](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)

Strands Decider (strands-labs/strands-decider):

- Qwen3.5-2B-Base torso with the LM head discarded and replaced by a pointer head of about one million parameters that compares the `<answer>` hidden state with each option's last-token hidden state, rank-16 LoRA on the torso, head in fp32. The README notes that the head has no per-option parameters, so "nothing can learn that 'the first option is usually right'" and the option count is uncapped. An earlier slot-head design "performed significantly worse". — [Strands Decider README](https://github.com/strands-labs/strands-decider)
- Reference model v1 (`strands-decider-2B-qwen3.5-v1-2610`) trains on 246,678 rows with gemma-4-31B-it as teacher and is a weight soup of three runs, then calibrated. Self-reported JevBench v1 public set (231 tasks): 0.779 accuracy, Brier 0.280, ECE 0.072. — [Strands Decider README](https://github.com/strands-labs/strands-decider)
- Loss (docs/architecture.md): cross-entropy on gold with 10% of a Score target's mass moved to neighbours, KL to the frozen torso's own readout (weight 0.3), and KL to a frozen teacher on multi-step rows (weight 1.0, the document names Qwen3.5-4B, while the README names gemma-4-31B-it as v1's teacher). — [Strands docs/architecture.md](https://github.com/strands-labs/strands-decider/blob/main/docs/architecture.md); [Strands Decider README](https://github.com/strands-labs/strands-decider)
- Training: `training/recipe.sh all` on one host with 1 to 8 GPUs builds the corpora, trains, calibrates and evaluates. "About 11 hours on one RTX 3090 (24 GiB), or 1 hour 10 minutes on eight H100s". `data/sources.md` lists every source and its licence. — [Strands Decider README](https://github.com/strands-labs/strands-decider)

PostHog Jeeves:

- Qwen3.5-9B, LoRA r=16 on all projections plus a pointer head (scaled dot product of a query projection at `<decide>` and a key projection at each `</opt>`), temperature-scaled softmax. The model reasons in a `<think>` block before deciding when its calibrated confidence is below 0.9. MIT. — [Jeeves README](https://github.com/PostHog/jeeves)
- SFT: one epoch, 16,867 steps on 4 GPUs, 453,531 questions from about 175 public sources. Calibration: two temperatures (1.52 direct, 5.42 after reasoning). v1 was SFT followed by CISPO, and "CISPO did not improve v2". `train.py cispo` and an 8-GPU `run.sh` are in the repository. The reasoning chains used for SFT are not published. — [Jeeves README](https://github.com/PostHog/jeeves)
- In `trainer.py`, the CISPO reward is the head's probability on the correct answer multiplied by penalties for unclosed or looping chains, with a group-mean baseline as advantage. — [Jeeves trainer.py](https://github.com/PostHog/jeeves/blob/main/trainer.py)
- Results (self-reported): JevBench overall on 231 public items v1 0.913, v2 0.896, Jev 0.866, JevK5 0.861. JevBench ECE on public items v2 0.059, v1 0.028, Jev 0.049, JevK5 0.014. About 20 ms per request without thinking and 1.2 s median with it on one H100 in FP8. — [Jeeves README](https://github.com/PostHog/jeeves)

Laya (Convai Innovations):

- Encoders, not decoders: `laya` on ModernBERT-large (421M, 512 tokens), `laya-multilingual` on mmBERT-base (322M, 1,024 tokens, up to 8,192), `laya-typed-decisions` on ModernBERT-large (421M, 1,024). Apache-2.0. Single forward pass, 32.8 ms per question on a T4 (self-reported). — [Laya README](https://github.com/NandhaKishorM/laya)
- The fine-tuning notebook runs on Kaggle's free 2xT4 and does "train with RLCD (proper-scoring-rule rewards, GRPO-style policy gradient), fit calibration temperatures, evaluate". — [Laya README](https://github.com/NandhaKishorM/laya)
- In `laya/train.py`, `rlcd_loss` adds zero-mean Gaussian noise to detached logits, rewards each of 4 noisy samples with `proper_reward` (a spherical-score and ranked-probability-score mix) against the target distribution, normalises the advantage and weights the Gaussian log-density, then adds soft cross-entropy. The module docstring says issue #741 "measured no gain from the extra term on the typed-decisions split". — [Laya laya/train.py](https://github.com/NandhaKishorM/laya/blob/main/laya/train.py)
- Results (self-reported): on typed-decisions (2,000 decisions) `laya-typed-decisions` 0.766 accuracy, Brier 0.062, ECE 0.213, against Jev 1.13.0's published 0.727, 0.148 and 0.144. On Banking77 Laya 0.425 against Jev 0.870, because options share a fixed `head_max_len` budget of 192 or 256 tokens. The README says both checkpoints "are over-confident as shipped". — [Laya README](https://github.com/NandhaKishorM/laya)

NanoJev, AgentJev and jevlike (small, game and agent oriented):

- NanoJev: Qwen3-0.6B backbone with decision heads. Choice uses set attention and a softmax over 2 to 255 candidates, Boolean a sigmoid, Score a probability-weighted level. Trained with complete-question cross-entropy on 18,760 questions per variant (ViZDoom, Maze, Snake), backbone learning rate 1e-5, head 1e-4. Self-reported ViZDoom Basic 128/128 against Jev 56/128. "RLCD post-training for broader long-horizon tasks" is an unchecked roadmap item. Model and data public on Hugging Face. — [NanoJev README](https://github.com/TianyuCodings/NanoJev)
- AgentJev-0.6B: Qwen3-0.6B with the LM head removed, a permutation-equivariant candidate head reading each candidate at its last token, 2,048-token context, states encoded once per request through a token trie. Self-reported on the typed-decisions test split: 79.25% top-1, Brier 0.0448, ECE 0.1687, against the published Laya checkpoint's 77.00%. ## Which projects can be trained on one to four university GPUs, and which have the cleanest code for a custom RL objective?

### Takeaway
Every 0.6B to 4B project with released training code fits one A100 or H100, and decider's documented RL stage used four GPUs only to run four parallel arms. For a custom RL objective, the cleanest starting points are Kev (smallest, best documented LoRA plus pointer-head trainer with Brier and ECE evaluation), Strands Decider (full recipe on 1 to 8 GPUs with KL anchors already in the loss), and rlcd-lite or Jeeves as the reference implementations of the RL loop, while decider gives the most complete written RL recipe but not its RL code.

### Cited Findings
- decider: SFT epoch 5.3 h on one GH200. Inference needs about 4 GB (2B), 8.4 GB (4B), 65 GB (35B bf16). The RL stage "needs ... four GPUs for the parallel arms". — [decider README](https://github.com/Mapika/decider); [decider docs/RL.md](https://github.com/Mapika/decider/blob/main/docs/RL.md)
- Kev: the first stage of Kev-0.8B takes about 20 minutes on one H100, Kev-4B about 1 hour on one H100, a sanity run about 1 minute, and `--batch 1 --accum 8` in bf16 fits Kev-0.8B on a 4 GB GPU. Kev-27B needs an 80 GB GPU to serve. A `kev-finetune` agent skill and six standard-library scripts implement the user fine-tune. — [Kev README](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)
- Strands Decider: about 11 hours on one RTX 3090 (24 GiB) or 1 hour 10 minutes on eight H100s with `FAST=1`, `training/recipe.sh all` for 1 to 8 GPUs. The README argues for 2B because "you can serve the model, and retrain the whole recipe, on hardware you already have". Every training run since v9 states predictions and failure conditions before training. 53 open issues. — [Strands Decider README](https://github.com/strands-labs/strands-decider); [GitHub API](https://github.com/strands-labs/strands-decider)
- Jeeves: SFT on 4 GPUs, published pipeline on 8 GPUs via `torchrun --nproc_per_node 8`, with SFT, CISPO and drafter stages in `trainer.py` and `train.py`. — [Jeeves README](https://github.com/PostHog/jeeves)
- Laya: fine-tuning notebook on Kaggle's free 2xT4 with DDP, and an Apple Silicon script. `laya.train.finetune(..., TrainConfig(...))` exposes `loss="rlcd"` or `loss="soft-ce"`. — [Laya README](https://github.com/NandhaKishorM/laya); [Laya laya/train.py](https://github.com/NandhaKishorM/laya/blob/main/laya/train.py)
- NanoJev trains a 0.6B backbone with head learning rate 1e-4 and backbone 1e-5, and its server needs CUDA. — [NanoJev README](https://github.com/TianyuCodings/NanoJev)
- JEV-27B training peaked at 79 GB on one B200 for about 9.2 GPU-hours, but its training code is not released. — [JEV-27B model card](https://huggingface.co/autotrust/JEV-27B)
- rlcd-lite trains Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with LoRA in about 7 minutes per 200-step arm on an M3 Pro laptop. mini-jev trains Qwen3-1.7B on a free Colab T4 (SFT and distillation about 1 h 45 min each, RL 2 to 3 h). — [rlcd-lite README](https://github.com/arnabgho/rlcd-lite); [mini-jev README](https://github.com/Shauryagulati/mini-jev)
- OpenSysOne, opendecider, OpenThai-SystemOne and openjev/openjev do not release training code (stated or absent from the repository). — [OpenSysOne reproduce.md](https://huggingface.co/andyshu/opensysone/blob/main/docs/reproduce.md); [opendecider tree](https://github.com/manjunathshiva/opendecider); [OpenThai-SystemOne card](https://huggingface.co/iapp/OpenThai-SystemOne); [openjev/openjev card](https://huggingface.co/openjev/openjev)
- Qwen3.5 bases mix attention with Gated DeltaNet layers that ignore attention masks, so Kev runs each question as its own row, and Kev recommends installing `flash-linear-attention` on CUDA. A Qwen3.5-0.8B prototype hit a DeltaNet kernel bottleneck on Turing GPUs. — [Kev README](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev); [qwen-rlcd README](https://github.com/shamazharikh/qwen-rlcd)

### Inferences
- On one to four A100 or H100 GPUs (40 to 80 GB) the trainable set is decider 0.8B/2B/4B, Kev 0.8B/4B/9B, Strands Decider 2B, AgentJev 0.6B, NanoJev 0.6B, Laya, Intern-Decision, Valen and Jeeves 9B (LoRA, 4 GPUs). Full fine-tunes at 27B (Kev-27B, JEV-27B) are at the edge of one 80 GB card and have no released training code in JEV-27B's case.
- For a one-step decision (one forward pass per environment step), the RL loop is REINFORCE or PPO with a group or leave-one-out baseline on the served softmax, as rlcd-lite and decider describe. That loop is short to write on top of Kev's or Strands' `forward()`, so the deciding factor is the quality of the model, data and evaluation code underneath, not the presence of an RL trainer.
- Jeeves' CISPO trainer optimises reasoning chains before the decision, which is a different policy from the one-pass decision the course project wants, so it is better used as reference infrastructure than as the base.
- Laya's "RLCD" term is a policy gradient over Gaussian-perturbed logits rewarded against a known target distribution, so it is a supervised proper-scoring objective in RL form, not RL from environment outcomes, and its own repository found no gain from it.

### Gaps
- No project reports A100-specific training times. Kev reports that the A100 is slower than the L40S for serving, which does not settle training cost.
- Memory use for PPO-style rollouts with a shell environment in the loop is not documented anywhere, because decider's RL code is unreleased.

## Ranking for the course project: a Jev-style decision model controlling an LLM agent harness, trained with a custom calibration-aware RL objective

### Takeaway
Mapika/decider-2b (or decider-4b) ranks first because it is the only open model whose released checkpoint was produced by outcome-reward, calibration-aware RL on agent environments with the loss computed on the served one-pass distribution, and its RL.md is a near-complete recipe to reimplement. Kev-0.8B or Kev-4B ranks second as the cleanest trainable base, and Strands Decider 2B third because it has the full public recipe and an existing measurement on shell-command gating. rlcd-lite and mini-jev should be read before writing the objective, because they document how proper-scoring rewards and GRPO normalisation break calibration.

### Cited Findings
- decider's RL stage used live MiniWoB++ click tasks where the page's elements are the options and the reward is ±1 from the task's checker, plus a belief question scored by a log score against the exact law, a rendering-consistency KL and a retention KL with drift gates. — [decider docs/RL.md](https://github.com/Mapika/decider/blob/main/docs/RL.md)
- decider's readout handles up to 255 options with a 32k context, and decider-2b needs about 4 GB of GPU memory. — [decider README](https://github.com/Mapika/decider)
- Kev's pointer head and `--init_from` fine-tune path, with Brier, calibration error and option-order metrics in `kev.benchmark`, train Kev-0.8B in about 20 minutes per stage on one H100. — [Kev README](https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev)
- tiny-bouncer measured zero-shot backends on a 265-command shell battery in the OpenCode harness: Jev reached FNR 0.000 and FPR 0.138 (three-way accuracy 0.845), and Strands Decider 2B (hobson-v21, local on an M1 Pro) reached FNR 0.000 and FPR 0.415 (three-way accuracy 0.766). Jev was the only backend to meet the project's hard gates. — [tiny-bouncer doc/findings.md](https://github.com/radupopescu/tiny-bouncer/blob/main/doc/findings.md)
- AgentJev's `coding_gates/` continuation is the only released fine-tune I found on real coding-agent outputs (test-runner logs), and it ships a Claude Code PreToolUse hook for Bash, Write and Edit. — [AgentJev README](https://github.com/malevrigns/agent-jev)
- Many Jev-based shell gates exist as applications (jev-auto-approve, jev-axi, jev-bouncer, blastgate on 881 real agent commands, and others), and all of them call hosted Jev rather than training a model. — [GitHub search API, 2026-11-01](https://github.com/BasmaAbouzied0/jev-auto-approve); [awesome-decision-models](https://github.com/AnotiaWang/awesome-decision-models)
- rlcd-lite found that rewarding Brier on stated confidence alone is exploitable and that RL collapses the raw softmax, and mini-jev found that GRPO's per-group standard-deviation normalisation inflates confidence. — [rlcd-lite README](https://github.com/arnabgho/rlcd-lite); [mini-jev README](https://github.com/Shauryagulati/mini-jev)
- LitJev's experimental System Two head routes individual questions to the frozen backbone's own slow thinking when it expects a gain, which is the same decision as the project's "fall back to an LLM" option. — [LitJev README](https://github.com/zhengxuyu/litjev)
- decider's README says rules written into the question are not followed at 2B ("a one-sentence question scores 0.67 and a paragraph of rules 0.24") and that a fixed convention has to be in the training data. — [decider README](https://github.com/Mapika/decider)

### Inferences
Ranking by suitability (the reader's task: at each step choose among a fixed list of shell commands, abort, flag danger, or fall back to an LLM, trained with the team's own calibration-aware RL objective on one to four GPUs):

1. Mapika/decider (2B, or 4B for more headroom). Its v10 recipe is already the project's design: a one-pass Choice over environment actions, PPO on the served softmax with leave-one-out baselines, a proper-scoring belief term, and KL retention gates, run on four GPUs. The SFT pipeline, data builders, temperature fitting and Brier, ECE and AURC evaluation are released under Apache-2.0. The cost is reimplementing the RL loop from RL.md, since that code is unreleased. Letter-slot readout means option order matters, so keep the command list in a fixed order or copy decider's two-order averaging.
2. Kev (0.8B for fast iteration, 4B for the final run). The cleanest small trainer: LoRA plus pointer head, one documented CE recipe, `--init_from` continuation, and a benchmark that already reports Brier, calibration error and order sensitivity. The pointer head has no per-option parameters, so commands can be added or removed without retraining a head. The team writes the RL loop, which for a one-step decision is a short REINFORCE or PPO wrapper around `DecisionModel` probabilities.
3. Strands Decider 2B. The same pointer-head design as Kev, a full public recipe that runs on 1 to 8 GPUs (about 11 hours on a 24 GB card), a loss that already includes KL anchors to the frozen torso (usable as the retention term of an RL objective), pre-registered experiment logs, and the only third-party measurement on a shell-command battery (zero-shot FNR 0, FPR 0.415). The FPR shows the base model over-flags safe commands, which is exactly what a calibration-aware RL stage would target.
4. AgentJev-0.6B. Small, Apache-2.0, coding-agent oriented (test-runner gates, Claude Code hook), with a permutation-equivariant head. Its 2,048-token context and smaller community are the limits. A good second baseline.
5. PostHog Jeeves 9B. The only released RL trainer (CISPO) inside a pointer-head decision model, useful as reference code for distributed rollouts and group-baseline advantages. Its RL acts on reasoning chains, its pipeline expects 8 GPUs, and CISPO did not help v2, so it is a poor base for a one-pass controller.
6. rlcd-lite and mini-jev. Not bases, but the two most useful readings for the objective itself, with ablations on Brier, binary and RLCR rewards, advantage normalisation, softmax collapse after RL, and the effect of adapter merging on ECE. rlcd-lite's 0.5B LoRA setup is a fast sandbox for testing the team's reward before scaling.
7. Laya. Cheap to train (2xT4) and has an "RLCD" loss in code, but its context is 512 to 1,024 tokens, accuracy collapses beyond about 20 options, its RL term is a supervised proper-score against a target, and its own repository measured no gain from it.
8. NanoJev. A 0.6B game controller with a clean CE pipeline and public data, but RL post-training is only a roadmap item and the code is game-specific.
9. Not recommended as a trainable base: OpenSysOne (no training code, custom checkpoint format, slower than its own verifier baseline), JEV-27B and JEV-9B (no training code, 27B, distilled from a closed model's outputs, so it inherits Jev's errors), opendecider (no training code), OpenThai-SystemOne (Thai-focused, no training code), openjev/openjev (non-commercial weights, no recipe), SiliconLabAI/OpenJev (a UI around decider). LitJev is a useful training-free baseline and its System Two router is a reference for the LLM-fallback action. CLM-8B is worth a baseline run because cached action embeddings fit a fixed command list, but its InfoNCE training is not an RL objective.

Two design consequences follow from the cited failure modes. The objective should keep a correctness or outcome term alongside any proper-scoring term on stated or implied confidence (rlcd-lite's Brier-only collapse), and it should use a mean-only or leave-one-out baseline rather than per-group standard-deviation normalisation (mini-jev), with a temperature refit after RL (rlcd-lite, decider, Jeeves). A retention KL to the SFT checkpoint with drift gates, as in decider's RL.md, guards general accuracy while the policy moves.

### Gaps
- No open project has been trained or evaluated on choosing among shell commands as actions (as opposed to gating a proposed command), so suitability for the action-selection framing is an inference from MiniWoB++ click selection (decider) and command gating (tiny-bouncer, AgentJev).
- Whether TypeSafe's terms of service restrict training on Jev outputs, which bears on JEV-27B, JEV-9B and mini-jev, was not checked.
- The licences of the datasets inside decider's and Kev's training mixtures were not audited, which matters only if the course project redistributes weights.
